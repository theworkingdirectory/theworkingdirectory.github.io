Book miniatures: Dostoevsky, White Nights and The Double

I re-read two stories from the collection that I already wrote about last year and will, of course, keep.

White Nights and The Double are two of Dostoevsky’s earliest stories slash novellas, written before Siberia and the mock execution, and they lack some of the all-pervading religious zeal of his later works. What makes them nonetheless recognizably Dostoevskyan are their main protagonists, who both incarnate the quintessential Dostoevskyan character that we later see again in Raskolnikov, the Underground Man, the protagonists of A Disgraceful Affair and The Eternal Husband, and many others: a man who is utterly and irredeemably pathetic. The two stories differ in how this makes us feel. The hero of White Nights is awkward, a loner and a loser, but when he realizes that the woman he loves does not love him back, and never did even though she said she did, and even though she genuinely tried to love him because she pities him, and that of course she cannot love him because he is just so utterly and irredeemably pathetic – when he realizes all of this, we sympathize with him. But the protagonist of The Double is worse: too despicable to like, and yet too innocent to hate. We feel we should pity him, but truth is we’d rather not think about him at all because he’s the kind of person who makes you physically uncomfortable.

Dostoevsky appears to take pleasure in all of this, in a masochistic kind of way. It’s as if he felt that he was utterly and irredeemably pathetic himself and was somehow obsessively compelled to remind himself of this fact. It made me think of those medieval saints who washed the wounds of lepers and then drank the water, indulging in suffering the way others indulge in gluttony. Some people seek out suffering in order to become impervious to it in the long term by building up a tolerance: if you remind yourself every day that you’re unpleasant, unlikeable and ridiculous, perhaps one day it won’t hurt so much anymore, and this makes quite a bit of sense. But others embrace suffering not because they want to feel it less but because they genuinely want to feel it more, and I do suspect that Dostoevsky was one of them. He transformed the awkwardness of everyday social life into a purgatory that he could pass through on his way to heaven.

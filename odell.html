Book miniatures: Odell, How to Do Nothing

This is a book that proposes a diagnosis and a treatment. The diagnosis: modern societies are suffering from a disease, and this disease are phones. Or, to be more precise, it is the constant presence of technology designed to captivate people’s attention in the form of push notifications, likes, endless scrolling, etc., not only harming their ability to interact with each other in meaningful ways, but also sucking the life out of life, as it were. People go through their day oblivious to their own experience. The treatment, according to Odell, is to slow down and pay attention. Rather than advocating some form of digital detox or retreating from society altogether, which she believes is neither feasible nor particularly helpful, she recommends activities such as birdwatching and deep listening which, although they may seem passive – the titular ‘doing nothing’ –, actually involve a deliberate and sustained engagement with the world around us. Her hope is that this can heal not only our souls, but also society as a whole. It is not exactly a novel proposal, but it is presented more convincingly than usual. In short, this is an edifying book.

I confess that I always get a perverse pleasure out of reading about digitally induced brain rot because I feel blissfully exempt from it. (The reader may have noticed my use of the third person in the previous paragraph.) I don’t use social media, I receive on average one interesting text or series of texts a day (or at least I did before I started dating on a regular basis), and I don’t really know how to waste time online other than by reading articles from the Well section of the New York Times, which are limited in number. (I enjoy these articles. They tackle important questions such as: Is green tea good for you? Is it better to exercise before or after breakfast? What is the best sunscreen to use? The response to all of these questions is invariably either a) we don’t know, b) it depends, or c) it probably doesn’t matter, which seems about right and makes me think that these articles are well researched.) I have what I believe is a fairly healthy relationship with my phone, so Odell’s entire argument at first did not seem to apply to me.

However, this is only true if the argument is understood very narrowly as being specifically about digital technologies. Here’s a fun fact. I cannot remember the last time I had a meal without reading a book or working at the same time. This might (or might not) seem like a virtuous habit, but at least in some respects, it is precisely the problem that Odell is concerned with. I am not paying attention to what I am doing because my attention is occupied elsewhere. What is more, I often find myself listening to music or podcasts when I could actually do with some quiet time, just because sitting on the bus or cooking or going to the supermarket without listening to anything feels like a waste of time. It is true that books, music and podcasts are far less addictive than, say, social media, and at least books require a more active engagement. However, they are all strategies for avoiding spending time with ourselves, and this seems to be the more fundamental issue.

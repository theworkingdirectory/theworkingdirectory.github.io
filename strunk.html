Book miniatures: Strunk, The Elements of Style

This hardly deserves to be called a book. It is precisely 41 pages long and little more than a collection of assorted rules for writing. It is, however, also very good, and so it deserves a post. I will simply summarize those rules that I want to be mindful of in the future.

- The word while should ideally only be used in a literal sense (i.e., during the time that).

- The word respectively is almost always unnecessary unless you are formulating a mathematical proof. (I purged at least five instances of it from my dissertation.)

- Coordinated ideas should be expressed in a similar way. If there is a parallelism in thought, then there should be a parallelism in form. Don’t be afraid of being repetitive.

- Avoid negation. Difficult is better than nontrivial (an adjective that I overuse in my academic writing). Obscure, vague, opaque and sometimes even hard to say are all better than unclear.

- Avoid the adverb very. There is almost always a better adjective that doesn’t need a modifier. Something that is very important can be decisive, crucial, paramount, etc.

- Use the word like with nouns and pronouns (like my mother, like her), and as with clauses (as my mother has done before).

- Lastly: omit needless words. The crucial question, of course, is which words are needless. I agree with Strunk on some points here, including that the question as to whether can usually be replaced by whether. But Strunk also objects to sentences like Spain is a country I would very much like to visit and recommends I would very much like to visit Spain. The first sentence is wordier, but it clearly establishes Spain as the topic of discourse, and so it is hard to say whether those additional words are indeed needless. At the end of the day, many such choices depend on whether you believe that you need to signpost your line of argument in painstaking detail for the reader at all times, or whether you believe that your reader is a grown-up who can make sense of more minimal prose.

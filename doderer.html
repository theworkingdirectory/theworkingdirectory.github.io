Book miniatures: Doderer, Die Dämonen

I made it to page 591 out of 1,345 before conceding defeat. In my defence – and of course, the only person who would think a defence is needed is myself –, Doderer’s German is dense and interspersed with Austrianisms, which slowed me down; as always, I was reading half a dozen books in parallel; and I haven’t had much time for reading at all over the past few months. And so every time I picked up Die Dämonen, a week or two had passed and I had once again lost track of the dozens of characters that inhabit Doderer’s world. It was not the right novel for this time in my life. I will need to return my copy to the library in less than two months when I’ll be leaving town, and I consider the chances of finishing it in that time to be approximately zero. Hence, I am conceding defeat.

I hardly ever do not finish a book that I started. I’m not a quitter. My reading is achievement-oriented to a fault. The only reason I’m writing this post is to feel like something is coming out of this after all. I could write about the connections to Dostoevsky, or Doderer’s very peculiar brand of conservative ideology, or some other interesting and meaningful idea I could extract from the book that would justify the time I spent reading it. Of course, all of this would prompt any well-meaning person to tell me to spend my free time in ways that I enjoy and not in ways that feel like a chore, and that there isn’t really any need to obsessively accumulate achievements in every area of my life. But I don’t really think this is an interesting response, and I’m not sure it’s true. I think people often misunderstand what achievements are all about.

Two days ago, I submitted my dissertation. This is as far as an objective achievement goes. I did not feel any euphoria or exhilaration or even relief. If I felt anything at all, it was a sense of loss: I had had a goal, and by achieving it, I lost it.

Later that day, I watched Boyhood. In one of the last scenes of the movies, our main protagonist is packing to move away for college while his mother sits in the kitchen. Suddenly, she starts crying and says, this is the worst day of my life. I had all those milestones, she says, getting married, having kids, getting my Master’s degree, finally getting the job I wanted. I sent your sister off to college, and now you. The next milestone will be my own funeral. She achieved all her goals, she met all her milestones, her life became emptier and emptier, and now she has nothing left. It is a strength of the movie that it doesn’t try to patch things up at this point. Her son reacts awkwardly, telling her that she is being a bit melodramatic; he doesn’t hug her like people in movies always do, and people in real life hardly ever. The last shot of the scene shows her still crying, and she says, I just thought there was more.

Book miniatures: La Rochefoucauld, Maximes

This is a collection of, you guessed it, maxims, located broadly in the ideological vicinity of people like Nietzsche and Gracián. La Rochefoucauld’s favorite idea, which he pushes relentlessly, is that most virtues are just vices in disguise: every seemingly noble act is undertaken for some selfish reason, appearance and reputation count more than true character, men are primarily driven by their desire to impress both others and themselves, and let’s not even get started on women. I can imagine that 350 years ago, all of this would have seemed highly insightful. I do not mean to say that any of it is wrong, just that it did not strike me as a particularly interesting perspective. This is the downside of being an influential thinker – you end up sounding like your own epigone.

The very last of the maxims did stick with me, however. It is by far the longest and I cannot quote it in full here. It is about death, and it says: don’t pretend that you aren’t afraid to die. If you think you aren’t, it just means that you haven’t sufficiently contemplated all the various forms in which death might come to you. People can get used to the idea of dying heroically in battle, but they would still be terrified of dying from cancer or a heart attack if they considered these possibilities. What is so deeply scary about death is not the pain, but the absolute randomness, and the fact that it could take you at any time. As an earlier maxim says: Le soleil ni la mort se peuvent regarder fixement – Neither the sun nor death can be looked at directly.
